SINKRONIZED / ARIZONA FIELD ATLAS

Arizona is our home.What happens beneath it matters.

Our cities, farms and desert communities depend on groundwater that lies out of sight. When water levels decline, the land above can slowly sink.

Sinkronized puts NASA’s new NISAR radar satellite to work on Arizona: we test its first year of data against a decade of older satellite records and published groundwater research, so the evidence is easier to explore—and its limits easier to understand.

WHY WE BUILT THIS

A familiar landscape. An overlooked change.

Arizona is where we live, learn and imagine our future. Understanding what is happening below the surface matters just as much as understanding what we can see above it.

01 / THE PLACE

A landscape we share

From expanding cities to agricultural valleys, our communities depend on groundwater. Much of that dependence is invisible in everyday life.

02 / THE CHANGE

Movement we rarely notice

In Arizona's alluvial basins, groundwater withdrawal can compact sediments, lower the ground surface and contribute to earth fissures. USGS research documents these changes.

03 / OUR RESPONSE

Make the evidence accessible

We built Sinkronized to connect radar measurements, observation dates and uncertainty with groundwater research—helping people ask more informed questions without pretending every pixel has a known cause.

The distinction matters: agriculture is a major estimated water-use sector in the Willcox Basin, and a 2023 peer-reviewed study links agricultural expansion with groundwater decline. That supports regional context, not attributing an individual satellite observation to a particular farm, well or development.

WHY THIS MATTERS

A clearer view of the ground we share.

Making the evidence understandable helps people see where closer investigation, monitoring and better-informed decisions may be needed.

WHAT WE ARE STUDYING

Not just whether the land moves. How confidently we can say so.

We investigate ground movement in the study areas supplied by our team’s current dataset. Satellite radar reveals changes along its viewing direction; real acquisition dates, stable reference areas, data quality and comparisons with Arizona Department of Water Resources research help us interpret those signals.

We distinguish the longer Sentinel-1 record from newer, preliminary NISAR measurements. Where the evidence is weak or missing, the website says so instead of presenting a guess as a result.

Why look in both directions? +
01 / ASCENDING South to north ↗

The satellite generally travels northward while observing the surface at a slanted angle.

02 / DESCENDING North to south ↘

The satellite generally travels southward and observes from a different slanted perspective.

Together, these views can strengthen confidence and help separate some motion components. One direction alone cannot establish a unique vertical sinking rate.

NASA NISAR / FIRST YEAR, TESTED

NASA’s newest radar satellite, tested on Arizona.

NISAR has revisited Arizona about every 12 days since late October 2025 (a few visits were skipped). We asked one plain question: does its first year see the same sinking that ten years of older satellite data show? These results are our own processing of NASA’s Provisional data.

How we know

Farmland can fool this radar

Published studies find that changing soil moisture and crops add apparent sinking to Sentinel-1-type (C-band) radar over fields: a systematic bias of up to several millimetres a year in general, and an estimated 2–4 cm a year for cotton in California, with the largest gaps against L-band in orchards such as pistachios, which also grow at Willcox. So part of the gap above may not be real sinking. What points the same way independently: NISAR’s L-band, less affected by this, also puts cropland lower (see below), and Willcox farmland has long been studied as a subsidence area.

Other cautions

Rain brightens every land type at once: NISAR’s scene-wide brightness follows the previous three days’ rainfall (rank correlation for HH, for HV; rainfall from the ERA5 weather reanalysis on a 25 km grid). So we compare crops with desert on the same date and re-checked the scores using only the dry dates. Not settled: NISAR’s own L-band rates also put cropland below desert ( cm/yr), but only cropland cells stay coherent all year, so we do not trust that size. The land-cover map describes the 2025 growing season while the radar year runs to November 2026, and fields rotate. None of this shows which farm, well or user caused any sinking.

GEOLOGY & INFRASTRUCTURE / ACROSS THE STUDY AREAS

What lies beneath, whether the ground comes back, and what it means for building.

This tab gathers what U.S. Geological Survey studies, Arizona law and the state water agency record about the ground under these basins. The geology differs from basin to basin: open a study area on the map to see what lies beneath that area. It summarises published findings. It is not engineering or legal advice.

01 / WHAT LIES BENEATH 140 ft of clay under Willcox Playa

Basins filled with sand, gravel and thick clay.

How we know

The Willcox basin covers about 1,500 square miles, of which the valley floor is about 950. It formed when large faults lifted the surrounding mountains, and it filled with gravel, sand, silt and clay washed down from them. Its main aquifer is lens-shaped layers of sand and gravel inside silt and clay. Under and around Willcox Playa lie the clay beds of ancient Lake Cochise. A test hole 140 feet deep at the centre of the playa found only black clay; along the playa’s east edge, clay continued more than 50 feet down. These clay beds hold water under pressure beneath them. Across south-central Arizona, the ground sinks most near basin centres, where these fine-grained layers are thickest. Sources: Brown and Schumann, USGS, 1969; Galloway and others, USGS, 1999.

02 / CAN IT COME BACK? Mostly no the sinking is permanent

Squeezed clay does not spring back.

How we know

While water levels stay above their previous lowest point, the ground only flexes and rebounds; in some basins it moves an inch or more with the seasons. Once levels fall below that point, the clay compacts for good. The USGS calls the lost storage “largely nonrecoverable”: the water squeezed out “cannot be reinstated by allowing water levels to recover.” Arizona shows it. In the Picacho Basin near Eloy, water levels rose by as much as 150 feet, yet some areas kept sinking, up to 0.3 inch a year, as the clays kept draining. In Willcox, a 2025 study found that recent wetter years gave only temporary, local recharge, while sinking persists and in some zones is speeding up. Sources: Galloway and others, USGS, 1999; Smilovsky, Geological Society of America, 2025.

03 / WHAT IT MEANS FOR BUILDING 12.5 ft of sinking near Eloy by 1977

Uneven sinking cracks, tilts and floods what we build.

How we know

By 1977, about 625 square miles around Eloy had sunk, by as much as 12.5 feet. Where the ground sinks unevenly, it can tear open earth fissures: long cracks that often appear after heavy storms and can erode into gullies more than 10 feet wide and 30 feet deep. They form mostly near basin edges and above buried bedrock “steps”. The USGS records damage to highways, railroads, sewers, canals, aqueducts, buildings and flood-control dikes in Arizona, including the Central Arizona Project canal where it crosses the Picacho fissure, and a natural-gas pipeline undercut by a fissure. Sinking also changes the slope of drains: near Luke Air Force Base a drain overflowed and flooded more than 100 houses. Willcox Basin has earth fissures too. Source: Galloway and others, USGS, 1999.

What planners and builders can do, from documented practice

  • Check the official fissure maps first. The Arizona Geological Survey maps earth fissures. By Arizona law (A.R.S. § 32-2117) the Department of Real Estate must post these maps, and the required notice says each map “supplements, and is not a substitute for, a professional inspection.”
  • Treat basin edges and buried bedrock steps as higher risk. That is where fissures concentrate. Long lines such as canals, pipelines and roads are most exposed where they cross them.
  • Design crossings for movement and erosion. Arizona examples: sheet piles on both sides of the CAP aqueduct, with the soil beneath compacted; and a road “land bridge” built from a 30-foot trench, compacted fill, a reinforced grid and a waterproof membrane.
  • Re-check drains and flood channels. Sinking changes their slopes and can make them overflow.
  • Monitor. Arizona agencies track sinking with borehole extensometers and GPS surveys; satellite radar adds wide coverage, and NISAR now provides free radar about every 12 days.
  • Slow the water-table decline. Where imported water, recharge and retired farmland stopped water-level declines, some fissures stopped being active. In Willcox the state designated an Active Management Area (order of December 19, 2024; in effect from January 8, 2025) after presenting evidence of land subsidence and fissuring. Irrigation is now limited to land already legally irrigated, and a draft goal aims to mitigate land subsidence by cutting groundwater overdraft at least 50% by 2075.
What this does not cover

This tab summarises published findings; it is not engineering, legal or real-estate advice, and any site decision needs a professional geotechnical investigation. The official fissure locations are not yet on our map: we could not download the Arizona Geological Survey dataset in time, so check its maps directly. The geology described is from boreholes and studies, not from our radar, which measures the surface only.
